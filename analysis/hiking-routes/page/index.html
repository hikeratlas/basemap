How much of the trail network is a route=hiking?

A first look at OpenStreetMap's hiking route relations in Canada, the United States and mainland France: how many there are, how much ground they cover, and which parks get them.

Route km counts each way once, even when several routes share it. Trails are highway=path/footway/bridleway/steps, minus sidewalks, crossings and private ways. Areas come from OSM's admin boundaries and include coastal and Great Lakes water, which inflates Canada's area most.

Counting relations misleads. Counting kilometres works better.

France has 44,000 hiking relations, three times the US count, but most of them are short local segments. 91% are tagged network=lwn, the median is 1.4 km, and only 36% have a name. A Canadian route is a named trail with a median length of 3.6 km. "Number of relations" mostly measures each community's mapping habits, so the rest of this page uses length.

Every French region beats almost every US state

Each dot is a state, province, territory or French region, placed by kilometres of hiking route per 1,000 km². The scale is logarithmic: each gridline is ten times the last. The least-mapped French region, Nouvelle-Aquitaine, has more route per km² than every US state except Connecticut and DC.

Correcting for size

Canada and the US have huge stretches of land with no trails, and route km per km² penalises them for it. A fairer question is how much of the trail people have already mapped is part of a route. Asked inside parks, so the comparison is like for like, France's 25× lead shrinks to about 2× for national parks.

Trail km in a route, typical park

Parks with a route

National parks look alike in Canada and the US: about 70% of those with trails have a route, and the typical one has 13–17% of its trail km in routes. The real gap is in smaller parks. Most state and provincial parks have no route, so the typical one scores 0%. In France, 80% of the réserves naturelles with trails have one.

Only parks with at least 5 km of mapped trail count, which drops trackless wilderness parks such as Quttinirpaaq and Wapusk. "Typical" is the median park, so a few large, heavily mapped parks can't dominate: added together, US national parks would show 37% instead of 13%. French routes mostly follow tracks, which these trail-only figures don't count, so they slightly understate France. For France the smaller parks are its réserves naturelles.

French routes walk on farm tracks and lanes

In North America about 70% of route length is on trails. In France it's 28%: sentiers balisés mostly follow forest and farm tracks and quiet roads between villages. So France's trail-coverage share (14%) understates how dense its waymarking is.

Famous parks get routes first

State and provincial parks, and France's réserves naturelles, with a Wikidata item, split into quarters by QRank, a ranking by Wikipedia page views. A park "has a route" if a hiking relation has a way inside it. Fame comes with size and trail mileage, so this is partly "big parks have more of everything". The gap is still large, and it holds in all three countries.

For France the comparison uses its 348 réserves naturelles, small protected sites with a median size of 1.2 km². France's 57 parcs naturels régionaux are left out: they are inhabited countryside covering about 18% of the country, and every one contains a route. So are its 8 national parks, which OSM maps as a core plus a larger buffer zone, merged here.

Fame isn't everything

Some very famous national parks have no hiking relation at all, even with hundreds of kilometres of mapped trail.

Other observations

  • Connecticut is an outlier. It has 2,699 relations, 17% of the US total, in a state smaller than Banff and Jasper together. 38% of its trail km are in a route, the highest of any state or province. Many are short colour-named trails ("White Trail", "Blue Trail") run by local land trusts and the Connecticut Forest & Park Association.
  • Prince Edward Island leads Canada at 30 route km per 1,000 km². The three territories together have 14 routes.
  • The South and the Plains are nearly empty. Texas has 50 relations and Mississippi 5, with only 2% of their trail km in routes.
  • Groups of routes are rare in North America. Relations that group other routes (a long trail's sections) number 39 in Canada and 97 in the US, against 504 in France.
  • Wikidata links are rare everywhere: 5% of Canadian routes, 2% of US and under 1% of French routes have one.
How this was made
  • Data: Geofabrik extracts of Canada, the US and France as of mid-November 2026, from Hiker Atlas's mirror, filtered to what its trail, park and route exports read. No Overpass queries.
  • A route is a route=hiking relation with at least one member way. It belongs to the region holding the midpoint of its longest way. Ways are placed by their midpoint. route=foot is excluded.
  • Parks are classed by name ("National Park", "Provincial Park", "State Park", "Parc national", "Parc naturel régional", "Réserve naturelle") and merged by name. A park whose centre point lies at sea is placed in the region it overlaps most. Québec's Sépaq "parcs nationaux" count as provincial.
  • Two boundaries were rebuilt by hand: Alaska, which is missing one maritime boundary way from the extract, and Grand Est, which has a small gap in its outer ring.
  • These are exploratory numbers. Expect a few percent of noise from park naming, region edges and extract borders.
